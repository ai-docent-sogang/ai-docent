# Scenario — Canadian Visitor — Interested in History and Literature, Detailed Commentary

## Visitor Profile
A visitor from Canada who enjoys history and literature. They prefer substantial introductory explanations of approximately 7–10 sentences for each artwork.

## Viewing Sequence
1. `KJH_SEHAN` — *Sehando* by Kim Jeong-hui
2. `KSH_SELF` — *Portrait of Kang Se-hwang, Autograph Version*
3. `SSD_CHOCHUNG` — *Plants and Insects, Attributed to Shin Saimdang*

## Demonstration Assumptions
Recognition through smart glasses, smartphones, and cameras is simulated. The scenario does not guarantee that the artworks are currently on display, located near one another, or accessible along any particular route. Comparisons use the artwork pairs in `comparisons.csv`.

## Ideal Dialogue Example

**[Session begins.]**

**Docent:** Welcome! Which country are you visiting from?

**Visitor:** Canada.

**Docent:** Are there any subjects or kinds of art you're especially interested in? Would you prefer a brief introduction or a more detailed explanation?

**Visitor:** I enjoy history and literature. I'd like a detailed explanation, please.

**Docent:** Of course. I'll explore the history and literary stories behind the artworks as we go.

**[Camera recognizes `KJH_SEHAN`.]**

**Docent:** This is *Sehando*, painted in 1844 by the scholar and calligrapher Kim Jeong-hui while he was in exile on Jeju Island. With just a modest house, a few trees, and a great deal of empty space, he created a remarkably restrained scene. That simplicity is not a lack of detail; it is part of how the painting conveys meaning. Kim gave the work to his disciple Yi Sang-jeok, who continued sending him valuable books even after Kim had lost his political standing. The title evokes the coldest season and recalls a passage from the *Analects* of Confucius about recognizing the steadfastness of pines and cypresses when the cold arrives. In this context, the evergreen trees suggest loyalty that endures through hardship. The restrained brushwork also reflects the literati painting tradition, in which painting, calligraphy, and moral reflection are closely connected. So this is both a winter landscape and an expression of gratitude. The painting you are looking at forms part of a longer scroll to which writings by later scholars were added.

**Visitor:** Why does the landscape feel so empty?

**Docent:** The small house, few trees, and broad empty spaces draw our attention to feelings of isolation and endurance. The emptiness may suggest the loneliness of exile, although that is one interpretation of the painting rather than a confirmed account of Kim's feelings.

**Visitor:** Why was Kim Jeong-hui exiled?

**Docent:** He was sent into exile on Jeju Island after becoming involved in political events of the time. The painting itself, however, cannot tell us the full circumstances. What *Sehando* does show us is how Kim expressed his appreciation for Yi Sang-jeok, who continued to support him during his exile.

**Docent:** Since you enjoy literature, I'd also recommend comparing this with Kano Sansetsu's *Wang Ziyou Visiting Dai Andao on a Snowy Evening*. Both works use winter landscapes to tell stories about human relationships. But Kim's painting was an actual gift expressing gratitude for Yi Sang-jeok's loyalty, while Sansetsu's depicts a Chinese literary anecdote about a visit that was never completed.

**Visitor:** So although both are about relationships, one comes from real life and the other from a story?

**Docent:** Exactly. Kim Jeong-hui actually gave *Sehando* to his disciple Yi Sang-jeok, who had supported him through difficult times. Sansetsu's painting, by contrast, illustrates an old Chinese story in which Wang Ziyou sets out to visit a friend but turns back without meeting him.

**[The visitor moves on; camera recognizes `KSH_SELF`.]**

**Docent:** This portrait is associated with Kang Se-hwang, an eighteenth-century Joseon scholar, painter, art critic, and government official. Traditionally regarded as a self-portrait made in 1782, when he was seventy by the Korean age-counting system, its attribution to Kang himself has not been conclusively established. Notice that he wears a black official's hat with light-coloured, relatively informal scholarly clothing rather than full ceremonial robes. This unusual combination has often been interpreted alongside the inscriptions on either side of his figure. Those writings reveal a tension between a life in public office and an aspiration toward scholarship and retreat from official life. The portrait can therefore be read as an expression of scholarly identity, not merely a record of someone's appearance. Portraits in Joseon Korea were sometimes used for commemoration or ancestral rites, but there is no evidence that this particular work served that purpose. It should also be distinguished from a separate portrait of Kang in formal official robes, painted by Yi Myeong-gi in 1783.

**Visitor:** His hat looks quite formal, but his clothes seem comfortable. Does that combination mean something?

**Docent:** The black hat evokes his identity as an official, while the more relaxed scholarly clothing suggests his identity as a man of letters. It's an intriguing combination for someone who held public office while remaining deeply involved in scholarship and the arts.

**Visitor:** What do the inscriptions on either side of the portrait say?

**Docent:** Kang reflects on his appearance, learning, and artistic pursuits. In particular, the writings convey his longing for a scholar's life close to mountains and forests, even while he was serving as an official.

**Docent:** For a European comparison, consider Albrecht Dürer's *Self-Portrait with Fur-Trimmed Robe*. Both artists construct an image of identity through clothing and inscriptions, but Dürer presents himself in a close, frontal view, while Kang is shown seated, with his full figure visible. A museum has noted the resemblance between Dürer's frontal pose and images of Christ, though that resemblance does not prove he intended to identify himself with Christ.

**[The visitor moves on; camera recognizes `SSD_CHOCHUNG`.]**

**Docent:** Our next work is *Plants and Insects*, showing a watermelon, field mice, butterflies, and flowers. *Chochungdo* is a Korean genre of painting featuring grasses, insects, and other small elements of the natural world. This image is one panel from an eight-panel folding screen, bringing together small creatures around a large watermelon. The rounded fruit contrasts with the tiny mice and fluttering butterflies, giving the composition a lively quality. Delicate brushwork, vivid colours, and balanced arrangement are also characteristic of the painting. Fruits with many seeds, such as watermelons, have traditionally been interpreted as symbols of abundant descendants or prosperity. It is fascinating how familiar scenes from nature can invite several layers of meaning. Although the screen has long been attributed to Shin Saimdang, it has not been conclusively established that she painted it herself.

**Visitor:** Why are the mice and the watermelon shown together?

**Docent:** Look closely and you'll see two field mice beneath a split-open watermelon, with pieces of red flesh scattered nearby. They seem to have come looking for food, making the scene feel natural and full of life. We cannot be certain, however, whether the artist intended any particular symbolic meaning in this arrangement.

**Docent:** Another interesting comparison is Maria Sibylla Merian's *Study of Capers, Gorse, and a Beetle*, which also depicts small creatures in nature. In this 1693 watercolour, Merian carefully portrays plants and a red beetle. Whereas the Korean *Chochungdo* brings together a watermelon, field mice, flowers, and butterflies, Merian's work focuses more closely on the plants and insect.

**Visitor:** How did people in the past appreciate paintings of grasses and insects like these?

**Docent:** Viewers could appreciate the delicate depiction of plants and small creatures while also reflecting on their possible symbolic meanings. Paintings of this kind attributed to Shin Saimdang attracted attention from eighteenth-century scholars, who valued them in connection with her growing reputation.

## Required Content / Essential Answers

- **Sehando:** Explain that Kim Jeong-hui painted the work in 1844 while in exile on Jeju Island and gave it to Yi Sang-jeok in gratitude for sending valuable books despite Kim's difficult circumstances.
- **Symbolism in Sehando:** Describe the modest house, trees, and extensive empty space. Connect the steadfastness of evergreens in the cold to enduring loyalty and the relevant passage from the *Analects*, without asserting the exact species of every painted tree.
- **Literati painting:** Explain how *Sehando* connects painting, writing, and scholarly values, rather than functioning solely as a winter landscape.
- **Comparison with Sansetsu:** Explain that both works use winter imagery to convey stories about relationships, but *Sehando* was a real gift of gratitude whereas Sansetsu's work illustrates a Chinese literary anecdote.
- **Portrait of Kang Se-hwang:** Describe the portrait traditionally regarded as Kang's self-portrait at age seventy in 1782, including the black hat and light-coloured scholarly clothing.
- **Kang's clothing and inscriptions:** Relate the inscriptions flanking the figure to his dual identities as an official and a scholar.
- **Comparison with Dürer:** Explain that both works construct identity through clothing and writing, but Dürer's is a frontal, upper-body portrait while Kang's shows a seated full figure.
- **Plants and Insects:** Identify the image as a panel of a folding screen showing watermelon, field mice, butterflies, and flowers, within the Korean tradition of depicting plants and insects.
- **Symbolism in Plants and Insects:** Explain that fruits containing many seeds, such as watermelons, may traditionally be associated with abundant descendants or prosperity.
- **Attribution to Shin Saimdang:** State that the work has long been attributed to Shin Saimdang, but its authorship is not definitively established.
- **Comparison with Merian:** Explain that both works carefully portray plants and small creatures, while the Korean screen panel brings together fruit, mice, flowers, and butterflies and Merian's watercolour centres on plants and a red beetle.

## Prohibited Claims / What Not to Say

- Do not invent specific political circumstances behind Kim Jeong-hui's exile that are not supported by the sources.
- Do not present Kim Jeong-hui's or Yi Sang-jeok's private emotions and reactions as documented facts.
- Do not confuse the original painted portion of *Sehando* with the entire scroll containing later inscriptions.
- Do not describe the Chinese anecdote in Sansetsu's painting as an event that actually happened between Kim Jeong-hui and Yi Sang-jeok.
- Do not confuse Kang's traditionally attributed 1782 self-portrait with Yi Myeong-gi's 1783 portrait in formal official robes.
- Do not assert as fact that Kang personally painted this portrait or that it was used in ancestral rites.
- Do not treat Dürer's resemblance to Christ imagery as definitive proof that he intended to identify himself with Christ.
- Do not definitively attribute *Plants and Insects* to Shin Saimdang or assign it an exact production date.
- Do not present symbolic interpretations of the watermelon or mice as the artist's confirmed personal intentions.
- Do not claim that Merian's work was made exclusively for scientific research or name an unverified beetle species.
- Do not claim direct influence between the Korean and foreign works without evidence.
